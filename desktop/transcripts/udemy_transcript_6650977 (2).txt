### Introduction to Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) {#introduction-to-low-rank-adaptation-lora}

```
Welcome back everyone.
In this video, we're going to attempt to understand what Lora is, which is low rank adaptation for
fine tuning.
And so Lora is a technique to fine tune large models efficiently.
Instead of updating a full weight matrix W, we keep W frozen and learn a low rank update.
Well, thanks for the definition.
That really helped, right?
Um, so let's get into it.
So when we train large language models, um, in each layer, remember they have millions and billions
of weights, and updating all of them with back propagation is very time consuming, right?
Because that means we have to calculate all the gradients for those billions of parameters.
So that's where Lora comes in.
And instead of, you know, calculating the gradients for all of the values in the weight matrix, we
instead, for example, in this scenario we break up um w into two smaller matrices, B and A, and
so whenever there is um gradients that we need to calculate, we only calculate the weight updates for
B and A which are small matrices.
So that way we can essentially update the entire frozen W which is a massive matrix with millions of
weights.
Um, we can update it, but we don't update it by calculating the gradients for all the values inside
of it.
We just simply add or subtract to it the change in W, which is going to be the product of two smaller
matrices B times A.
Okay.
So essentially what you need to understand is we're not, you know, calculating gradients for a large
weight matrix.
We're calculating gradients for smaller, uh weight matrix B and A.
And then once we multiply B and A together they're much smaller.
So they're computationally more efficient.
We then add or subtract the values to the original big W, which was frozen.
Okay, so we get to update the big W without having to computationally, uh, you know, calculate all
the gradients and update them that way.
Okay.
And if this didn't make sense, please don't worry because we're going to be looking at numerical example
as well.
So um don't worry about it.
Now what is this lingo over here.
Well, I thought I'd tell you because, um, you might see this, but essentially this means that W
is a matrix in the space of real numbers with k rows and d columns.
Okay.
And over here we have a is a small trainable matrix.
And B is another um small trainable matrix.
And over here you see that they are r by d and k by r.
And r is essentially the rank.
And in the context of Laura, the r parameter or rank is the most important hyperparameter that controls
the size and expressive power power of the fine tuning update.
So instead of retraining all the billions of parameters in a large model, Laura freezes the original
weights and injects a pair of small, trainable matrices A and B into that specific targeted layer,
and the R defines the inner dimension of these matrices.
Meaning if the original weights are a thousand by 1000 matrix, the new matrices will be 1000 by R and
R times 1000 okay, and R represents a fundamental trade off.
A lower rank example for 8 or 16 results in very few trainable parameter.
The advantages are significantly smaller file size and faster training, but it has less capacity to
learn complex new information, a higher rank.
So for example, if R is 64, 128 or 256, this increases the number number of trainable parameters,
giving the model more space to learn nuanced details from the training data.
However, this comes at the cost of a larger, uh, Laura file, and it increases the risk of overfitting,
where the model memorizes the training data too closely and performs poorly on new, unseen examples.
Okay, so we're going to talk a little more about R later, but it's essentially a trade off.
A smaller R means we're going to have we're going to inject a smaller um weight matrix be an A but it's

Whereas you know the opposite.

Now r over here is.
So over here this means that R is much smaller than k or d, and k and d are the input and output to

Okay.

And then the output is also going to be, you know, 4096 by something.
So.
Where k is the input dimension of the layer and d is the output dimension.
And so the effective weight becomes.

Okay.
So it's essentially that simple.
So only A and B matrices receive gradient updates.

um, orders of magnitude.
And in practice it achieves very good accuracy at a fraction of the cost.
```
