=== Bring Data Into Your Data Warehouse ===


--- What is a Data Warehouse? ---

Instructor: Hi, everybody.
In this video, we'll take a first look
at the concept of data warehousing.
In the most basic sense,
a data warehouse is literally that,
a fairly large warehouse,
but one that's filled with data
rather than physical products.
It's important to note that a data warehouse
is not the same as a database,
even though the names sound familiar.
There is a relationship, though.
A data warehouse is typically built
on top of some type of a database,
so you can think of a data warehouse as the usage
and the database as the platform.
It's also important to note
that the data inside of a data warehouse
comes from elsewhere.
In other words, our operational systems
and sometimes also external sources.
In other words, we don't create data for the first time
in a data warehouse as part of some type of a transaction.
Buying something online,
or a student registering for a class,
or maybe a new faculty member being hired at a university,
those transactions occur
and are recorded in various operational systems,
and their data is then subsequently sent down
to the data warehouse.
Many data warehouses have dozens of data sources
and you can also assume
that there's a linear relationship in play here.
The more sources, the more complex the overall environments.
Another critical point is that data is copied, not moved.
In other words, the data remains in our source systems
and then copies are made and sent into the data warehouse.
A data warehouse is more than just a storage location
for lots of data, though.
There are rules that govern how we build our data warehouses
and how we organize and store our data.
Way back in 1990, a gentleman named Bill Inmon
came up with a set of rules for data warehousing.
The first of which
is that a data warehouse is an integrated environment.
In other words,
data from a number of different source systems
are sent into a data warehouse.
The second rule is that a data warehouse
should be subject-oriented.
Regardless of how many systems
and which data is coming from which systems,
we need to reorganize the data by subjects,
and we'll take a look at that throughout this course.
The third rule technically is called time variant,
but really that's just a fancy way of saying
that our data warehouses contain historical data,
not just current data.
And then the fourth rule is really the most complicated one
for data warehousing newcomers to understand,
non-volatility.
Traditionally, we will periodically load data
into a data warehouse.
Think of it as refreshing a data warehouse
to keep it current,
and we do so in batches.
Let's just say that once a day,
we'll grab all of the new and updated data
from source applications
and copy that data into the data warehouse.
Between the time of the last refresh and the next one,
the data warehouse stays as is,
even if thousands, or perhaps even millions,
of transactions are occurring in our transactional systems.
That's what we mean by non-volatile.
In other words, for traditional data warehousing usage,
our data warehouse remains stable in between refreshes.
So we can then do things like strategic planning
without the data changing underneath us.
A final point to make is that as we bring the data
into the data warehouse,
we'll typically restructure and reorganize it
to make it more useful for analysis.
And that brings us to the final rule
from the early days of data warehousing
that's still with us.
Why do we even build these environments
that are integrated, subject-oriented,
time variants, and non-volatile,
and which pool data from our transactional systems?
And the reason is to support data-driven decision-making.

--- Compare a Data Warehouse to a Data lake ---

Instructor: It's sometimes confusing to differentiate
between the idea of a data warehouse,
which goes back to the early 1990s,
and the more recent term data lake.
Both are valuable tools.
So let's take a look at the similarities
and differences between the two.
A data warehouse is often built
on top of a relational database
such as Microsoft SQL Server, Oracle, or IBM's Db2.
Relational databases are also used
for transactional systems and applications,
not just data warehousing.
So you can think of a relational database
as sort of a Swiss army knife,
kind of a general purpose data management engine
that's useful in various settings.
Sometimes we also build a data warehouse
on top of a multidimensional database
that's typically known as a cube.
We'll look at cubes later, but for now,
think of a cube as sort of a special purpose database,
not just a relational one.
In contrast, a data lake is built
on top of some sort of big data environment
rather than a traditional relational database.
Big data and its usage in data lakes
helps us manage extremely large volumes of data,
larger than we typically would include
even in the largest data warehouses.
Volume is one of the traditional three V's of big data.
Big data also supports very rapid intake of new
and changed data much more rapidly
than we typically do with traditional data warehousing.
And finally, the third V
along with volume and velocity is variety.
Our traditional data warehouses mostly work
with structured data, numbers, dates,
shorter length character strings, and similar data types,
which big data easily supports
along with semi-structured data such as text messages,
emails, blogs, and complex documents,
as well as unstructured data such as audio and video.
We're sometimes able to handle
those other types of data in our data warehouses,
but we usually need to do some special data tricks
such as using blob data types in certain database engines.
Some people think of data lakes
as the next generation of data warehousing
and there is some truth to that point of view.
The three V's capabilities certainly open up possibilities
not only for expanded data management,
but likewise expanded decision-making capabilities.
At the same time though,
most organizations still have very robust
data warehousing environments,
even as they invest in big data technology
to build data lakes.
In fact, the lines between the two are increasingly blurred.
SQL, S-Q-L, the standard relational database language
provides not only the backbone
of traditional BI, business intelligence,
but also could be layered on top of a big data environment.
From a user perspective, traditional BI can then be done
against either a data warehouse or a data lake.
In fact, we can revisit our earlier depiction
of the synergies between business intelligence
and data warehousing and add data lakes into the mix.
All in the pursuit of data-driven decision making.


=== Managing Data Warehouse History Through Slowly Changing Dimensions ===


--- Compare a Data Warehouse to Data Virtualization ---

Instructor: Another discipline
that is sometimes confused with data warehousing
in addition to data lakes is data virtualization.
To understand data virtualization,
we actually need to take a brief look back
into the history of data warehousing.
In the days before data warehousing came onto the scene,
data-driven decision making was largely done
by building what we often called extract files
that were exactly as they sounded,
sort of like mini-data warehouses
pulling data from one or more applications,
but without any coordination of data structures,
rules, or pretty much anything else.
Far more time was spent gathering and reorganizing data
over and over than actually analyzing data.
In the late 1980s,
most of the major computer systems companies of the day
were working on something
called distributed database management systems.
Think about an internet search engine,
but instead of building a monster-sized index
for websites and web pages,
the index would instead point to applications,
and their databases, and specific data.
In theory, a user would write queries
against a distributed database
regardless of how dispersed the actual data was,
and the index would then reach out to the various places,
pull in their correct data,
organize it, and present the result to the user.
Without going into details,
the idea of distributed databases was mostly a failure.
The concept was a solid one,
and in fact, in some ways a distributed database
is very much a earlier incarnation of how Hadoop works
as a big data engine
with its data distributed among many underlying nodes.
However, the technology of the late 1980s
and even into the very early 1990s
wasn't nearly powerful enough
to support the distributed usage of data
the way we were trying to make it work.
That led us to two diversion paths.
One path took us into what became data warehousing.
The idea was that if we can't reach out
and grab data on demand
in its original applications and databases,
let's preemptively pull what we think we need
and then, as we saw earlier,
integrate copies of that data together
into some sort of a totally separate environment.
At the same time, though,
another school of thought wasn't all that happy
with this idea of copying data
and attempted to modify the idea of distributed databases
into what is now known as data virtualization.
Essentially, data virtualization
can be thought of as a read-only distributed database
versus the original distributed database idea
of not only reading the data,
but also updating it in place
via those indexes that we were building.
The key point for data virtualization
is that unlike data warehousing,
we do not copy the data into a separate database.
Instead, we access it from its original locations
at the time we need to do so for reports and analytics.
Data virtualization has actually had many names
over the years for essentially the same concept
such as virtual data warehousing,
enterprise information integration, or EII,
enterprise data access, or EDA,
and now most of the time we know it as data virtualization.
The technology that we use for data virtualization
has significantly improved
from the late 1980s and the early 1990s,
which makes it much more useful and successful
than its earlier incarnations.
Having said that though,
it's best to think of data virtualization
as being particularly useful in certain use cases.
If we have data that requires simple transformations
or perhaps even no transformations
to be used in BI and analytics,
and if we have only a handful of data sources,
and also if we have some leeway
on the response time to our queries and reports
then data virtualization
can be part of our overall portfolio
along with data warehousing and data lakes
to help deliver data-driven decision making
through business intelligence and analytics.
