=== Section 1: Foundations of LLM Evaluation ===


--- Types of evaluations – intrinsic vs extrinsic ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to talk about the two main types of evaluations for large language models
intrinsic and extrinsic.
This distinction is important because it shapes the way we understand a model's performance.
Let's begin with intrinsic evaluation.
This type of evaluation focuses on the model in isolation without looking at any specific application.
For example, we might calculate the perplexity of the model to check how well it predicts the next
word in a sequence.
Or we could measure benchmark scores like Belle Vue or accuracy on a data set.
These methods are quick and easy to automate, and they provide standardized results that allow us to
compare models.
However, the limitation is that intrinsic evaluation doesn't always reflect how the model performs
in the real world.
In other words, a model can score very high on a benchmark but still fail to deliver useful answers
to real users.
And this is exactly where extrinsic evaluation comes in.
Instead of testing the model in isolation.
Extrinsic evaluation looks at how the model performs within a real application or task.
For instance, imagine we are using an LM inside a customer support chatbot.
Here we don't just care about benchmark accuracy.
Instead, we measure whether the chatbot actually solves customer problems, how satisfied users feel
with the interaction, and even how fast the responses are.
This approach is more realistic because it reflects the true value the model provides in practice.
At the same time, it is also more challenging because it requires human feedback.
Real tasks and often continuous monitoring.
So to connect the two ideas, intrinsic evaluation asks the question does the model generate good outputs
by itself?
While extrinsic evaluation asks does the model help solve real world problems effectively?
Both types are necessary and they complement each other.
Intrinsic methods give us efficiency and comparability, while extrinsic methods give us relevance and
impact.
That is why in practice, a strong evaluation strategy usually combines both approaches.

What makes a large language model good?
This is not just about having the latest or the largest model.

Accuracy means that the model produces correct and reliable answers.

Helpfulness is about providing answers that are not only correct, but also relevant, clear, and actionable.

This is what makes the model feel like a real assistant rather than just a database of information.
Next, we need to consider safety.
Safety ensures that the model avoids harmful, biased or inappropriate outputs.

Imagine a chatbot giving financial or medical advice without proper safeguards.
The risks can be significant.
So a good model must include mechanisms to reduce harmful content and maintain ethical standards.
Finally, let's talk about latency.
Latency refers to how quickly the model responds, even if the answers are accurate, helpful, and

Users will get frustrated if they have to wait too long.
For example, in a live chat bot for e-commerce, a delay of several seconds can reduce customer satisfaction
and even impact sales.
So speed is a key part of the user experience.
To connect all these points together, we can say that a good LLM balances these four dimensions.
It is accurate, it is helpful, it is safe and it is fast.

=== Section 3: Systematic Error Analysis ===


--- Challenges in evaluating generative outputs ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to discuss the main challenges in evaluating generative outputs from large
language models.
This is a critical topic because unlike traditional software, the outputs of Llms are not always predictable
or easy to score.
The first challenge is subjectivity.
When an LLM generates text, different people may judge the quality in different ways.
For example, if the model writes a summary of a news article, one person might find it clear and concise,
while another person might think it is missing key details.
This subjectivity makes evaluation inconsistent and harder to standardize.
Closely related to this is the challenge of open endedness.
In many cases, there is not just one correct answer.
For instance, if you ask a model to write an introduction to machine learning, there could be dozens
of different valid responses, each with a different style, structure, or level of detail.
Unlike a math problem, there is no single ground truth, which makes automatic scoring very difficult.
Another big challenge is context sensitivity.
The quality of a response often depends on the situation or the user's specific needs.
For example, if a user asks about Python functions.
A beginner might want a simple explanation with an example, while an advanced developer might expect
details about decorators and performance.
The same answer could be rated as excellent by one user and completely unhelpful by another.
Finally, there is the problem of scalability.
Human evaluation remains the gold standard, but it is costly, time consuming, and does not scale
well when thousands of outputs need to be checked.
Imagine a system generating millions of responses every day.
It would be impossible to have humans review all of them.
This is why automation, LLC based judges and sampling strategies are often introduced.
Even if they are not perfect to bring all these points together, evaluating generative outputs is challenging
because we are dealing with subjective judgments, open ended answers, context dependent quality,
and the high cost of human review.
These challenges explain why evaluation in the world is still an active research area, and why new
methods are constantly being explored.


=== Section 4: Evaluation Techniques & LLM-Judge Approaches ===


--- Logging LLM inputs, outputs, and metadata ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to talk about one of the most important practices in evaluating ML systems,
which is logging inputs, outputs and metadata.
This is the foundation of systematic error analysis because without proper logs, we cannot understand
why a model failed or how to improve it.
Let's begin with the inputs.
Inputs are everything that goes into the model.
This includes the user's prompt, the system instructions, and any context documents that were passed
along.
It also includes model parameters such as the temperature, maximum tokens or sampling settings like
top k and top P.
For example, if a user asks the chatbot to summarize this article in three bullet points, the log
should contain the article itself, the prompt, and the parameter values used.
By saving all these inputs, we can later reproduce the exact same conditions when we investigate an
error.
Now let's move to the outputs.
Outputs are the responses generated by the model.
Logging them means capturing the full text that was returned and if available, any confidence scores
or ranking values.
This is essential because when a model produces an incorrect, biased or unsafe response, we need to
have that output recorded for review.
For example, if a customer service chatbot incorrectly explains a refund policy, the logged output
allows us to trace the issue, compare it to the input, and decide how to retrain or adjust the system.
In short, outputs give us the evidence of what the model actually said.
Next, we need to talk about the metadata.
Metadata is the surrounding context of the interaction.
It includes technical details like timestamps, latency, token usage, and even the API version used.
It can also include user related information such as the session ID, the user's role, or the application
context.
For example, if we notice that latency spikes during peak traffic hours, metadata provides the evidence
to confirm this pattern.
Another case could be when errors only happen for certain user roles.
Without metadata, we would never see these correlations.
So when we combine inputs, outputs and metadata, we create a complete trace of each interaction with
the model.
This trace allows us to reproduce errors, analyze conditions, and continuously improve performance.
Without it, we are essentially blind, unable to explain or fix problems in a systematic way.

=== Section 7: Human Review & Cost Optimization ===


--- Categorizing LLM failures (hallucinations, bias, toxicity) ---

Hello everyone.
And this slide we are going to talk about the different categories of failures that large language models
can produce.
Understanding these categories is important because it allows us to identify patterns and design specific
solutions, instead of treating all errors the same way.
The first category is hallucinations.
This happens when the model generates content that sounds convincing but is completely false or fabricated.
For example, a model might invent a research paper that doesn't exist or give a wrong statistic when
summarizing data.
Hallucinations are dangerous because the text often looks credible, and users may trust it without
verifying the facts.
The second category is bias.
Llms are trained on large datasets that contain human language, and unfortunately, human language
often carries stereotypes and prejudices.
As a result, the model may generate outputs that are biased against gender, race, or other social
groups.
For instance, if an LLM is asked for hiring suggestions and it favors one gender over another without
justification, That is, a biased output bias can reduce fairness and damage trust in the system.
The third category is toxicity.
This refers to harmful or unsafe language such as offensive comments, abusive responses, or dangerous
advice.
For example, a chatbot that responds aggressively to a user or one that provides unsafe instructions
about handling medication falls under toxicity.
This category is especially critical because toxic outputs can create serious harm for users and organizations.
So why do we need to categorize failures?
Because each type requires a different mitigation strategy.
Hallucinations can be reduced with grounding techniques like retrieval.
Augmented generation bias can be mitigated through data curation and fairness checks, and toxicity
can be controlled with safety filters, moderation layers, and clear guardrails.
To sum up, failures in llms often fall into three main categories hallucinations, bias, and toxicity.
By categorizing them, we can move from a vague understanding of the model made a mistake to a structured
process of diagnosing problems and applying targeted solutions.

--- Root cause analysis frameworks ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to talk about root cause analysis frameworks and why they are so important
for evaluating large language models.
When something goes wrong, it is not enough to just note the error.
We need to understand why it happened and how to prevent it from happening again.
Let's start with the purpose of root cause analysis.
The idea is to go beyond surface level symptoms and uncover the underlying reasons for a failure.
For example, if a chatbot produces a hallucinated fact, the symptom is the answer was wrong, but
the root cause could be missing data in the training set.
Or maybe the retrieval pipeline didn't return the right documents.
By identifying the root cause, we can design a solution that prevents the same error from repeating.
There are several frameworks we can use.
The first one is the five whys technique.
This is a simple method where we keep asking why until we arrive at the true cause.
For instance, why was the output wrong?
Because the document was missing.
Why was the document missing?
Because the retrieval index was outdated.
Why was it outdated?
Because the update job failed by the fifth.
Why?
We have reached the actionable problem.
Another useful tool is the fishbone diagram, also called the Ishikawa diagram.
This framework helps categorize possible causes into groups such as data issues, model limitations,
deployment errors, or even user behavior.
For example, a toxic response could come from biased training data, from weak filtering in the deployment
layer, or even from a user deliberately prompting in a harmful way.
The fishbone diagram helps visualize these different categories.
Finally, we have fault tree analysis.
This method maps how multiple smaller issues can combine into a bigger failure.
For example, high latency might result from a combination of network delays, large prompt sizes,
and inefficient token generation.
Fault tree analysis shows how these factors connect and lead to the observed problem.
So to summarize, root cause analysis is about diagnosing problems systematically instead of just saying
the model failed.
We use frameworks like the five whys, fishbone diagrams, or fault tree analysis to trace failures
back to their true origins.
This way, we can not only fix the current error, but also design systems that avoid similar issues
in the future.

--- Feedback loops and error logging strategies ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to focus on feedback loops and error logging strategies.
These two elements are at the heart of continuous improvement, because they ensure that every error
becomes an opportunity to learn and improve the system.
Let's begin with the idea of a feedback loop in practice.
No system is perfect, so what matters is how quickly it can adapt.
Feedback loops allow us to capture signals from users and from the system itself, and then feed those
signals back into improvements.
Without feedback loops, mistakes are repeated again and again.
There are two main types of feedback.
The first is explicit feedback, where users directly tell us what they think.
For example, thumbs up and thumbs down buttons, short surveys, or rating scales after a chat bot
interaction.
This is very clear feedback, but users don't always take the time to provide it.
The second type is implicit feedback, which is often just as valuable.
This includes signals like when a user repeats the same question, abandons a session quickly, or spends
longer than expected to reach a solution.
For example, if many users re-ask the same query in different words.
That is a strong signal that the initial answer was not satisfying.
Now let's connect this to error logging strategies.
When errors occur, we should log them with a full trace including the input, output and metadata.
Each error should be tagged by category.
For example, hallucination, bias, toxicity or latency issue.
By tagging, we can later identify which problems are the most frequent and which have the greatest
impact.
This prioritization helps teams focus on solving the issues that matter most.
Finally, we need to talk about closing the loop.
It's not enough to just collect feedback and errors.
We must use them.
This means feeding error data back into retraining models, optimizing prompts, or strengthening guardrails.
It also requires regular reviews to ensure that fixes are effective and that failure rates are going
down over time.
So to summarize, feedback loops capture both explicit and implicit user signals, while error logging
strategies systematically record and categorize failures.
When combined, they turn raw errors into actionable insights that drive continuous improvement in LM
systems.

--- Human evaluation vs automatic evaluation ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to compare human evaluation and automatic evaluation of large language models.
Both approaches are widely used, but each has strengths and weaknesses that make them suitable for
different situations.
Let's start with human evaluation.
This means that real people review the outputs of the model and judge them based on criteria such as
quality, helpfulness, or safety.
For example, if a model generates a medical explanation, a human evaluator can assess whether the
explanation is clear, accurate, and appropriate for the audience.
The advantage of human evaluation is that people bring nuance and contextual understanding that automated
metrics often miss.
However, the downside is that human evaluation is expensive, time consuming, and cannot easily scale
when thousands of outputs need to be checked.
Now let's look at automatic evaluation.
This approach uses predefined metrics to measure model performance.
For example, metrics like blue or rouge are commonly used in summarization tasks.
While perplexity is often used to measure how well a model predicts sequences.
The big advantage is that automatic evaluation is fast, scalable, and provides standardized results.
But the limitation is that it often fails to capture subjective qualities like tone, clarity, or usefulness.
For example, a summary could score well on rouge, but still feel confusing or incomplete to a human
reader.
So how do we decide between the two?
The reality is that both methods have their place.
Human evaluation provides depth and nuance, while automatic evaluation provides scale and speed.
That's why the best practice in evaluation is usually to combine both approaches.
For instance, automatic metrics can be used to screen large volumes of outputs, and human reviewers
can then focus on the most critical or ambiguous cases.
To sum up, human evaluation and automatic evaluation are not competitors, but complementary tools.
Together, they provide a more complete and reliable view of performance.

--- Using LLMs to grade other LLMs (LLM-as-a-judge techniques) ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to explore a fascinating method called law as a judge, where one language
model is used to evaluate the outputs of another.
This approach has gained popularity because it promises both scalability and flexibility in evaluation.
So what does it mean in practice?
Imagine we have a model generating answers to user questions, and instead of asking a human to rate
each answer, we use another law to act as a grader.
The judge model can be asked to assign a score, rank multiple outputs, or provide feedback on dimensions
such as accuracy, clarity, or helpfulness.
For example, if two different models generate summaries of an article, a judge LLM can compare them
and decide which one is clearer or more complete.
The advantages of this approach are clear.
First, it is highly scalable.
A judge can evaluate thousands of outputs quickly, something that would be impossible with human reviewers
alone.
Second, it is flexible since we can design prompts that instruct the judge to look for specific qualities
such as relevance, tone, or style.
And third, it is more cost effective compared to paying for large scale human annotation.
However, there are also important limitations.
A judge may carry the same biases as the models it is evaluating, which means it could reinforce unfair
patterns instead of correcting them.
There is also the risk of hallucinated judgments where the judge provides inconsistent or unjustified
scores.
And finally, while useful judges are still not as reliable as expert human evaluators, especially
in critical domains like healthcare or law.
So what are the best practices when using law as judges?
One strategy is to combine them with human spot checks, where a subset of outputs is reviewed by humans
to ensure calibration.
Another is to design clear evaluation prompts that reduce ambiguity for the judge model.
And finally, using multiple judge models and aggregating their results can improve reliability.
In summary, as a judge, techniques provide a powerful way to scale evaluation, but they should not
replace humans entirely.
Instead, they work best when integrated into a hybrid system that combines automation with human oversight.

--- Pairwise comparison and scoring methods ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to discuss two important evaluation methods pairwise comparison and scoring.
Both techniques are widely used to measure the quality of outputs from large language models, but they
work in slightly different ways.
Let's start with pairwise comparison.
In this approach, we take two outputs, usually from two different models or two different versions
of the same model, and we compare them side by side.
The evaluator, whether a human or an LLM as a judge, then chooses which output is better.
For example, if we are testing two summarization models, we show both summaries of the same article
and simply ask which one is clearer and more accurate.
This method is very effective for a B testing because it forces a direct comparison.
Now let's move to scoring methods.
Instead of choosing between two options, the evaluator assigns a numerical score to a single output,
often on a scale like 1 to 5.
The output can be scored across multiple dimensions such as accuracy, clarity, safety, or helpfulness.
For example, a chatbot answer might receive a four out of five for accuracy, but only a two out of
five for clarity.
By aggregating these scores across many outputs, we can build detailed metrics that show where the
model performs well and where it struggles.
Both approaches have advantages and limitations.
Pairwise comparison reduces subjectivity because it is often easier to decide which of two outputs is
better than to assign a precise score.
However, pairwise only tells us relative quality.
It doesn't say whether either output is actually good.
On the other hand, scoring gives us fine grained insights across different evaluation dimensions,
but it can suffer from inconsistency if different evaluators interpret the scale differently.
So what is the best practice?
In reality, these two methods are complementary.
Pairwise comparison works well when we need relative comparisons between systems, while scoring is
better for building detailed quality profiles across dimensions.
By combining both approaches, organizations can achieve a more balanced and reliable evaluation of
outputs.

--- What makes Retrieval-Augmented Generation different? ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to discuss what makes retrieval augmented generation or R.a.g different from
standard.
This is an important concept because Rag has quickly become one of the most common ways to make language
models more reliable and useful.
Let's begin with a simple definition.
R stands for retrieval augmented generation.
It is a framework that combines two components.
First, a retriever, which searches for relevant documents or knowledge from external sources, and
second, a generator, which is the LL that produces the final answer by using the retrieved information
as context.
Now why is this different from plain LL?
A standard LL relies only on the data it was trained on, which means its knowledge is fixed at training
time.
This can lead to hallucinations or outdated answers.
For example, if you ask a plain ll about the latest research paper published last month, it won't
know because it has no access to fresh data.
With Rag, the retriever can pull in recent documents and the generator can integrate them into the
response, making the answer both more factual and more up to date.
There are several advantages to this approach.
First, Rag helps reduce hallucinations by grounding responses in real documents.
Second, it allows domain specific customization.
For instance, a Rag system for healthcare can search medical literature before generating answers.
And third, it improves trustworthiness since users can often be shown the sources that were retrieved.
But of course, there are also challenges.
If the retriever brings back irrelevant or wrong documents, then even the best generator will produce
a poor answer.
There is also integration complexity.
Because the retriever and the generator must work seamlessly together to provide consistent results.
So to summarize, Rag is different from plain llms because it adds a retrieval layer that supplies the
model with relevant real world data at the time of the query.
This makes the system more factual and reliable, but also introduces new challenges that must be carefully
evaluated.

--- Evaluating retrieval quality (recall, precision, relevance) ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to discuss how to evaluate the quality of retrieval in retrieval augmented
generation systems.
This is a crucial step because if the retrieval component fails, the generation component will almost
always produce a weak or misleading answer.
Let's begin with recall.
Recall measures how many of the truly relevant documents were actually retrieved by the system.
For example, if there are ten relevant papers in the database and the retriever brings back eight of
them, then recall is 80%.
High recall means the system is not missing important information.
The risk of low recall is that the generator might have to work with incomplete context, which can
easily lead to hallucinations or oversimplified answers.
Now let's move to precision.
Precision looks at the other side.
Out of all the documents retrieved, how many are truly relevant?
For example, if the retriever returns ten documents and only seven are relevant, then precision is
70% high.
Precision means users are not overloaded with irrelevant content.
Low precision, on the other hand, can confuse the generator because it mixes useful data with noisy
or unrelated material beyond recall and precision.
We also need to consider relevance.
This is more qualitative.
It's not just about numbers, but about the actual usefulness of the retrieved documents.
For instance, imagine a user asks what are the latest techniques in cancer treatment?
The retriever might return a paper that mentions cancer but is focused on diagnostics rather than treatment.
Technically, it's related, but it doesn't fully answer the query.
So relevance is low.
So why is all of this important?
Because in rag, poor retrieval equals poor generation.
If the retriever fails to bring back the right information, the l.l.m. will produce an answer that
is inaccurate or incomplete.
No matter how advanced the model is.
On the other hand, strong retrieval ensures that answers are factual, grounded, and trustworthy.
To sum up, evaluating retrieval quality means looking at recall, precision, and relevance.
Together, these metrics provide a clear picture of how well the retrieval system is supporting the
generation process.

Precision looks at the other side.
Out of all the documents retrieved, how many are truly relevant?
For example, if the retrieval returns ten documents and only seven are relevant, then precision is
70% high.
Precision means users are not overloaded with irrelevant content.
Low precision, on the other hand, can confuse the generator because it mixes useful data with noisy
or unrelated material beyond recall and precision.
We also need to consider relevance.
This is more qualitative.
It's not just about numbers, but about the actual usefulness of the retrieved documents.
For instance, imagine a user asks what are the latest techniques in cancer treatment?
The retriever might return a paper that mentions cancer but is focused on diagnostics rather than treatment.
Technically, it's related, but it doesn't fully answer the query.
So relevance is low.
So why is all of this important?
Because in rag, poor retrieval equals poor generation.
If the retriever fails to bring back the right information, the LL will produce an answer that is inaccurate
or incomplete.
No matter how advanced the model is.
On the other hand, strong retrieval ensures that answers are factual, grounded, and trustworthy.
To sum up, evaluating retrieval quality means looking at recall, precision, and relevance.
Together, these metrics provide a clear picture of how well the retrieval system is supporting the
generation process.

--- Designing evaluation in production environments ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to talk about designing evaluation in production environments.
This is a crucial step because evaluating a model only in the lab is not enough.
Real world conditions often expose issues that benchmarks cannot capture.
Let's start with the why.
In controlled tests, we measure performance using benchmarks and synthetic datasets.
But in production, models face real users, unpredictable inputs, and much larger scale.
For example, a chatbot may work perfectly on internal test prompts, but struggle when customers ask
questions in slang, abbreviations, or mixed languages.
This is why evaluation in production is essential.
It tells us how the model behaves under actual use.
Now let's look at the key considerations.
First we need continuous logging of inputs, outputs and metadata.
This allows teams to analyze failures later and reproduce issues if needed.
Second, we need real time monitoring.
This includes tracking latency, error rates, and even safety violations as they happen.
For instance, if toxic content is generated, the system should flag it immediately instead of waiting
for offline review.
Third, we should collect user feedback directly from the production system.
This can be explicit feedback like thumbs up and down, or implicit signals like whether users abandon
the conversation quickly.
These signals are extremely valuable for continuous evaluation.
Of course, there are trade offs.
Running heavy evaluation in production can slow down the system or increase costs.
We also need to consider privacy and compliance when storing logs and user data, especially in regulated
industries like healthcare or finance.
So to summarize, designing evaluation in production means creating a framework that runs live continuously
and safely.
It requires logging, monitoring, and feedback collection, all while balancing performance, cost,
and privacy.

--- Integrating eval into CI/CD or workflow pipelines ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to talk about integrating evaluation into CI, CD and workflow pipelines.
This is important because deploying models without automated evaluation is risky.
Even small updates can introduce regressions that harm user experience.
Let's start with the why.
In traditional software engineering, CI CD pipelines ensure that every code change is automatically
tested before going live.
The same principle applies to systems.
We need automated evaluation steps so that issues are detected early, not after deployment.
For example, a new model version might have slightly better accuracy, but suddenly generates slower
responses.
Without automated checks, this could go unnoticed until users complain.
Now let's look at the key steps.
The first step is running pre-deployment checks, where we evaluate the new model on benchmarks before
release.
This ensures that accuracy, safety, and efficiency meet the required standards.
The second step is automated testing.
Here, evaluation scripts are integrated directly into the CI CD Pipeline checking metrics such as latency,
token usage and safety thresholds automatically.
This way, if a change fails, it is blocked before deployment.
The third step is canary deployments.
Instead of rolling out a new model to everyone, we release it to a small percentage of users first.
We then monitor its behavior in real world conditions and if no issues are found, we expand to full
deployment.
This reduces risk and gives us more confidence in the update.
Now let's connect this to workflow integration in MLOps pipelines.
Evaluation should not just happen once.
It should be embedded into both staging and production environments, using monitoring tools like Prometheus
or Grafana to continuously track performance after deployment.
This way, evaluation is not a separate step.
It is part of the development life cycle itself.
To summarize, integrating evaluation into CI CD pipelines ensures early detection of problems, continuous
quality assurance, and faster iteration cycles.
It allows teams to innovate quickly while maintaining reliability and user trust.

--- Alerting, thresholds, and incident response ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to cover alerting thresholds and incident response in production evaluation.
This is where monitoring turns into action, because detecting a problem is only useful if the right
people are alerted quickly and know how to respond.
Let's begin with why alerts are needed.
Even with strong monitoring in place, issues can still occur in production.
Latency might suddenly spike, token usage could skyrocket, or the model might produce unsafe outputs.
If we wait for users to report these problems, the damage is already done.
Alerts allow us to detect anomalies early and take action before users are heavily affected.
Now let's talk about thresholds.
Thresholds define the acceptable limits for system performance and safety.
For example, we might set a rule that if latency exceeds three seconds for more than 5% of requests,
an alert is triggered, or if the system's toxicity score rises above 0.2, the response is flagged
for review.
By defining thresholds, we turn raw metrics into meaningful signals that indicate when something is
going wrong.
Next, we need to consider the alerting systems themselves.
Popular tools like Prometheus and Grafana can monitor metrics and generate alerts.
Pagerduty and slack integrations are often used to notify engineers directly, but one key principle
is to make alerts actionable and prioritized.
If the system sends hundreds of alerts every hour, engineers will start ignoring them.
This is called alert fatigue, so alerts must be designed to focus on what truly matters.
Finally, let's move to incident response.
When an alert fires, there must be a clear playbook that defines how to respond.
This includes steps like rolling back to a previous model version, temporarily disabling a risky feature,
or escalating the issue to the right team.
Equally important is clear ownership.
Everyone should know who is responsible for handling alerts and how quickly they must respond.
So to summarize, alerting thresholds and incident response ensure that when issues occur in production,
they are detected quickly, escalated properly and resolved efficiently.
This minimizes downtime, maintains reliability, and most importantly, protects user trust in the
system.

--- Creating scalable human-in-the-loop review systems ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to discuss how to create scalable human in the loop review systems.
This is essential because while automated evaluation is powerful, it cannot replace the nuanced judgment
that humans provide.
Let's begin with the why automated metrics.
And as a judge, systems can evaluate outputs at scale, but they often miss subtle issues.
For example, they might score an answer as accurate even though it contains a misleading tone or a
cultural bias.
Humans, on the other hand, are much better at catching these edge cases, especially in sensitive
areas like healthcare, finance, or education.
That's why human review remains a critical part of evaluation.
Of course, human review has challenges.
It is expensive, slow and very difficult to scale when dealing with thousands or millions of outputs.
If every single response had to be reviewed manually, the system would quickly become unsustainable.
So how do we make it scalable?
One method is sampling.
Instead of reviewing all outputs, we only review a statistically significant subset.
For example, checking 5% of chatbot conversations each day can already provide a reliable view of overall
quality.
Another method is triage, focusing human review on high risk scenarios.
For instance, an e-commerce chatbot giving delivery information may not need human review, but a medical
chatbot suggesting treatments definitely does.
By prioritizing critical use cases, we use human time where it matters most.
Finally, we can build hybrid systems.
This means using automated checks for routine cases like flagging toxic language or latency issues,
while reserving human reviewers for complex or sensitive outputs.
This hybrid approach balances efficiency and depth.
So to summarize, scalable human in the loop systems rely on sampling, triage, and hybrid designs.
The best practice is to involve humans only where their judgment adds the most value, while letting
automation handle the rest.

--- Balancing eval quality vs budget constraints ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to talk about balancing evaluation quality against budget constraints.
This is a key challenge because while we want accurate and reliable evaluation, we also need to make
sure the process remains affordable.
Let's begin with the trade off.
On one side, high quality human review provides deep insights and nuanced judgments, but it is expensive
and slow.
On the other side, automated checks are much cheaper and faster, but they can miss subtle issues like
tone, fairness, or context.
This creates a constant balancing act between depth and cost.
So how do we manage this balance?
One approach is sampling.
Instead of reviewing all outputs, we review only a fraction.
For example, reviewing 10% of chatbot sessions may already give a good picture of performance while
keeping costs under control.
Another approach is tiered evaluation.
This means adjusting the level of review based on the domain and risk.
For instance, legal or medical outputs may require heavy human review, while product recommendation
systems may need far less.
This allows organizations to focus resources where mistakes would be most damaging.
Finally, there is the hybrid approach.
Here we combine automated scoring with human spot checks.
Automation handles large scale tasks like detecting latency spikes or toxic outputs, while humans step
in to review cases where judgment and nuance are required to make this effective.
Teams should also use cost aware metrics.
This means prioritizing the metrics that have the biggest impact on business outcomes like user satisfaction,
safety, or compliance.
Spending heavily on low impact metrics.
For example, fine tuning minor stylistic issues is rarely worth the investment.
So, to summarize, balancing quality and budget requires smart allocation of resources by using sampling,
tiered evaluation, and hybrid systems.
Organizations can achieve reliable evaluation without overspending.

--- Token and model selection strategies to reduce costs ---

Hello everyone.
In this slide we are going to talk about token and model selection strategies to reduce costs.
This is a very practical part of cost optimization, because most expenses are directly tied to how
many tokens we use and which model we choose.
Let's begin with token efficiency.
Every word we send to and receive from an LLM is counted as tokens, and costs increase with the number
of tokens used.
One way to reduce this cost is by designing shorter, more efficient prompts.
For example, instead of pasting a full customer history into every query, we can summarize it and
only provide the essential context.
Another strategy is to truncate or compress long conversations before feeding them back into the model.
These techniques keep token counts lower while still preserving the key information.
Now let's talk about model selection.
Not every task requires the most powerful and expensive model for routing queries like FAQ answers or
simple text classification.
A lightweight model such as GPT 3.5 is often sufficient, but for complex reasoning safety, critical
tasks, or high stakes decision making.
Larger models like GPT four or Claude Opus are more appropriate.
We can also explore open source models, which can be fine tuned for specific domains and deployed at
lower cost.
Another powerful idea is using hybrid strategies.
This means routing queries intelligently.
Simple requests are handled by a cheaper model, while only the complex or high value queries are sent
to the advanced model.
For example, a customer support system might use a lightweight model for delivery status questions,
but escalate to a stronger model for refund policy or legal queries.
In addition, retrieval augmented generation can reduce token usage by retrieving only the most relevant
documents instead of passing long contexts.
So, to summarize, token and model strategies are about matching resources to the task by keeping prompts
efficient, choosing the right model for the job, and using hybrid strategies, we can minimize costs
without sacrificing quality.
