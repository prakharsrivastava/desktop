=== Typography in Slide Design ===


--- 2.2 Workflow for Slide Design ---

Hello everyone!
In the previous lecture, we explored the process of creating presentation slides by breaking it down
into two key stages content writing and slide design.
We took an in-depth look at the three steps involved in content writing.
In this session, we will focus on the three stage workflow for slide design.
Before diving into the details, let's first discuss why we break slide design into these three stages
to create persuasive and professional presentation slides.
It's important to adhere to a few key principles.
First, the design must be relevant to the content.
The closer the slide design aligns with the content it presents, the more effectively the message will
be conveyed.
For example, if you're designing slides for a presentation about a VR device, the design should reflect
the product's concept to enhance both engagement and credibility.
Second, maintaining design consistency is crucial.
A presentation usually consists of multiple slides, and as the number of slides increases, it becomes
more important to keep the design consistent.
This consistency ensures that the audience can intuitively understand the information without needing
to adapt to new designs and layouts with each slide transition.
Third, it's essential to incorporate visual interest.
The longer a presentation, the more likely the audience's attention may wane.
To counteract this, visual interest should be incorporated at key points in the presentation.
This might involve using transition effects in slide design to momentarily recapture the audience's
focus, particularly when introducing significant content or shifts in the topic.
Lastly, productivity and workflow is key.
The design process should be as productive as possible to reduce the time spent on slide creation,
thereby enhancing overall project efficiency.
To uphold these four key principles of slide design, the workflow is divided into the following three
stages.
The first stage is to create a mood board.
A mood board is a is a single slide that compiles the core elements that will define the overall tone
and style of the slide design.
This includes fonts, image styles, color palettes, and infographic elements.
Starting with a mood board is highly efficient for several reasons.
By establishing a clear design concept that aligns with the content from the outset, this concept then
serves as a consistent guideline for the design of all slides, ensuring visual uniformity throughout
the presentation.
A unified slide design allows the audience to grasp the content more quickly and intuitively, without
needing to adjust to new design elements with each slide transition.
Furthermore, when adding new slides, you can simply duplicate or format copy elements from the mood
board, significantly speeding up the design process compared to inserting new graphic elements from
scratch.
Once the mood board is complete, the next step is to design the layouts for key slides.
A typical presentation consists of the following main slides.
By designing these five types of layouts in advance, you can modify and adapt them for the remaining
slides.
This approach not only helps maintain design consistency, but also improves workflow efficiency.
After completing the layout design for all slides, the final stage is to apply effects such as morphing,
which can effectively maintain audience engagement, especially in longer presentations.
However, these transitions should be used strategically to draw the audience's attention to the speaker
and the key messages.
Overusing such effects can distract from the content itself, reducing the overall impact of your presentation.
By following this three stage workflow, you can easily adhere to the four key principles of slide design.
In the next lecture, we will apply these three stages to create an actual presentation slide.
That's all for this session.
I'll see you in the next lecture.

--- 5.1 Two Principles of Color Utilization ---

Hello, everyone.
Welcome to the fifth chapter of our presentation Design course.
In this session, we'll focus on color utilization, which is a core element of presentation design.
Many people struggle with selecting colors, questioning the criteria for choosing background or text
colors.
They also often feel unsure about how to effectively combine different colors.
To tackle these challenges, let's first explore the fundamental criteria for using colors in presentation
slides.
As we discussed in our previous sessions, all design decisions for slides stem from one critical objective
enhancing the persuasiveness of the message.
To break this down further, this means selecting colors that semantically align with the slides message
and combining colors in a way that ensures optimal readability and effective communication.
So how do we choose colors that truly complement our message?
Colors inherently convey unique emotions and atmospheres, which can vary slightly across different
cultural contexts, but there are also universally understood emotional responses.
Therefore, it's crucial to understand the emotional impact.
Each color communicates and how to harmoniously connect this with the slides topic.
For example, warm colors can communicate passion and energy, while cool colors can convey stability
and trust.
By carefully selecting colors that align with your slides theme, and purpose, you can significantly
enhance your design's impact.
Let's dive deeper into the emotional language of colors.
Colors can be broadly categorized into two main groups achromatic and chromatic colors.
Achromatic colors lack saturation and are defined solely by their lightness.
These include white, black, and shades of gray.
These neutral colors enhance message delivery with their simplicity and sophistication, reinforcing
the intended meaning or emotion.
This is why many online stores use achromatic tones in their designs using variations of black, white
