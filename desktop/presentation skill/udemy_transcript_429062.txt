--- The Five Steps of Effective Delegation ---

When you're delegating a task to one or more persons, here are five proven steps to use.
Make sure you prepare the steps beforehand and that you have written down key points for the person
to be able to review after your meeting.
Step one specify outcome.
Be clear and specific with your desired result.
Begin with the end in mind.
Explain the task clearly and describe goals, not actions.
Ask the person you are delegating to how they have understood the desired result.
This will help avoid common misunderstandings and later frustration.
Also, set a clear timeline including deadlines.
Step two guidelines or framework establish controls and limits to the work.
Clearly identify boundaries and constraints are the organizational or financial limitations that need
to be adhered to.
Also clarify what responsibilities and what authorities come with the performance of this task.
Should the person wait to be told what to do?
Ask what to do.
Recommend what should be done and then act or take action and then report periodically.
Step three resources.
What kind of support can they expect from you, from other people?
Provide sufficient support, but resist upward delegation.
If there is a problem.
Don't let the person shift responsibility for the task back to you.
Instead, ask them for recommended solutions and don't simply provide an answer.
What other resources are available for them for solving this task?
This could include courses or books to study, machines to use, and available software.
Step four evaluation or monitoring.
As you delegate the task, agree on a schedule of checkpoints on which you will check in with and review
progress.
When the person or persons have started work on the delegated task.
Keep up with the progress.
When you do check in, focus on results rather than procedures.
Make adjustments as needed.
Step five feedback and recognition.
When the work is done, give recognition where it is deserved.
This step is easily missed in the everyday business of things, so do not get sloppy here.
It makes a big difference for future motivation and productive behavior.
If there were aspects of the result or the work that did not meet your expectations, provide constructive
feedback or coach them on how they can improve in the future.

Three.

--- Introduction to Coaching ---

It so happens that we mean different things when we talk about coaching, some mean a way of talking

With the deepest respect for that, we can actually mean different things when we use the word coaching.

Coaching is learning to ask questions to get people to move more effectively from A to B.
Many things could be said about why coaching is useful, but let's use a metaphor to illustrate the

Let's imagine a village by the sea that is lacking food, and therefore the people living there are

Coaching is about that final approach, providing resources and creating opportunities.
This often happens through the use of questions allowing people to learn themselves.

--- A Tested and Proven Format for Coaching ---

This way of coaching is used on a floor space with four papers laid out on the floor.
If you do not have that kind of floor space available, and the four papers can be symbolized by, for
example, four pens that you have taped the corresponding words onto.
When the person you're coaching is in a certain position, he or she grips the corresponding pen and
it then becomes more of a mental experience.
The floor setup is preferable if you have the space, since it gives a clearer experience of the four
positions.
Place for A4 or letter sized papers with the following text on the floor according to this illustration.
Note that the papers obstacles and solutions resources lay closer to each other on purpose compared
to the other two, as if they are by the side of an imaginary road from present situation to goal desired
situation.
It can also be useful that you as a coach, have paper and pen handy during the coaching sessions,
for example to write down questions you want to ask later in the session, or something that the other
person says.
That seems extra important.
Step one ask the person you're going to coach to stand on the paper.
Goal desired situation.
Ask him or her to describe the answer to the question what is it that you want?
Make sure the description is vivid and full of detail.
Ask the person to enter the experience of the goal as much as possible.
This is called being associated with the experience in the experience fully.
Your job here is to help out with questions that help the person experience more fully, physically
and emotionally, already being in their desired situation.
One way of seeing whether the person is associated is to note his or her breath.
A more forceful and intense breathing is a sign of being more in the experience.
Step two ask the person to stand on the paper.
Present situation ask what does it look like today?
Make sure the person describes the present situation factually and without much emotion involved.
The objective here is to get the person to be dissociated, as if he or she was sitting in a helicopter,
looking down at the situation.
You could also ask them to see the situation as if it was a black and white movie.
Taking the color out of a situation can sometimes make it less emotional.
Otherwise, it happens easily that the person gets stuck in the emotions and is not able to neutrally
describe the different aspects of the present situation.
This dissociation can in itself be a help for many people to see the situation in a different way.
Step three invite the person to stand on the paper obstacles.
Ask them to look at the path from present situation to goal desired situation and answer the following
question.
What is stopping you from being at the goal already today?
Make sure the person tells you all the obstacles they are able to currently see.
Then it is time to move to step number four.
Step four ask the person to stand on the paper.
Solutions and resources.
Let them answer the following questions.
What resources do you have for getting past these obstacles?
Here it is about finding positive things that can contribute.
Please note that it does not have to be complete solutions.
You as a coach can help the person discover more resources.
You could ask questions such as could your curiosity be a resource here?
Or could your stubbornness be useful here?
Resources can also be in the form of somebody the person could call to get help along the way.
It is extra important in this step that you are mindful of the person's body language, and help him
or her with this.
For example, if his or her shoulders drop while looking down at the floor, it is hard to find the
mental and emotional state to see opportunities and solutions.
This step is also a lot about increasing the will to action.
Step five.
In this step, you first invite the person to go back to the paper obstacles and see which new obstacles
that have showed up.
Often when we pull out resources and solutions, new obstacles appear in relation to this.
Perhaps an obstacle could be about how the person will be able to get in contact with somebody they
are intending to call, or how they will find the time to make use of their stubbornness.
In this step, let the person move back and forth between the papers, obstacles and solutions and resources
according to the instructions in step three and four.
Keep doing this until he or she no longer is able to see any obstacles on the path from the present
situation to goal desired situation.
Step six.
Invite the person to stand on the paper.
Goal.
Desired situation again.
Ask the person to feel really feel fully associated with the experience, how it is to stand there and
know that nothing stops him or her from getting there.
Use the 1 to 10 closing question from the previous lecture to make sure the person truly believes the
goal will happen if they are not at a nine or a ten.
Invite them back to the obstacles paper and ask them what stops it from being a ten.
Then redo steps 3 to 5 until they are able to give you a nine or a ten at step six.

--- Decide by Yourself or In a Group - Practical Tips ---

When making a decision, it is important to ask yourself if it is a decision best made by yourself or

If people come into it thinking they will make the actual decision together with you, but you only

Effectiveness.

--- Which Domain Are You Making Your Decision In? ---

When making a decision, it's important to understand the context in which the decision is made within

approach to make a decision.
The decision making framework was developed by Dave Snowden based on complexity.
Science can have him, by the way, is a Welsh word meaning habitat.
Snowden explains that different problem situations warrant different approaches to find the right solution.
Having a good solution to a problem is great, but applying it in the wrong context can lead to outcomes
that are overly complicated or harmful.
To simplify the frameworks usage further, Johnstone and Berger from Cultivating leadership added the
distinction between what is predictable and what is unpredictable.
In a predictable world, we can expect what will happen.
It happened before and will most likely happen again.
While in an unpredictable world, things do not necessarily happen again in the way they happen before.
The Carnarvon Framework puts the context of decision making into one of four main domains.
These domains then guide how to approach the problem.
The four domains are obvious.
Complicated.
Complex.
Chaotic.
Let's take a closer look at each of these.
Obvious.
Obvious contexts are characterized by stability and cost effect relationships that are clear to everyone.
Often the right answer is self-evident.
The connection to why it happens is predictable, repeatable, and there's often a best practice.
Here you sense what is going on, put it into the appropriate category, and then decide which action
to take.
If you can drive one car, you can drive another of a different brand.
If you follow a recipe, your meal will most likely turn out the same given you use the same ingredients.
Complicated, complicated contexts may contain multiple right answers, and though there is a clear
relationship between cause and effect, not everyone can see it.
It is not too obvious, and you need knowledge and expertise to understand what to do, as things are
not exactly the same as last time.
There is no best practice and experts might propose different solutions, though there may be a good
practice.
In other words, here you first sense and then analyze before making a decision on how to move forward.
Let's use the example of developing new technical solutions that no one has developed before, like
an electric airplane.
It is a new technology, though the design can be based in parts on previous concepts such as electric
cars.
You have experts with different areas of expertise in the project, and together they complete the new
airplane complex in complex context.
The right answers can't be figured out beforehand, simply because it has not happened this way before.

You need to probe and sense before making a decision on how to act.

People.
Falls into this domain, as you cannot use the same leadership approach in all situations or with all

Chaotic.
In chaotic contexts, searching for the right answer is pointless.

This is the area of unknowables and requires novel practice.

A fifth domain disorder.

--- Examples of Six Biases that can Derail Your Decision Making ---

In this video, we will talk briefly about some common biases.
As mentioned before, there are many different biases, but here are a few that are most likely to interfere
with your decision making confirmation bias.
Description the tendency to search for, focus on, and remember information that confirms your existing
beliefs.
This can lead you to miss relevant information.
Example you believe that one of your coworkers is lazy.
When a team fails a project, the other members point their finger at the lazy person as the cause for
the team's shortcomings.
This confirms your belief that the person is lazy and you desire to fire the lazy person.
In reality, the team displays interpersonal issues that have more to do with the other members of the
team than the lazy one, and you are now stuck with a dysfunctional team that is also lacking the competence
the lazy person had.
How to manage.
Find ways to challenge what you think you see.
Discuss your thoughts with others, especially ones with different views than yourself.
Seek information from a variety of sources before making a decision.
Play the devil's advocate and contest your potential choice in as many ways as possible.
Anchoring bias description.
When you rely too heavily on one trait or piece of information, often something that comes up early
in the process.
Think of it as a first impression bias that you anchor in.
Example.
If you first receive a tender from a potential supplier that offers you the parts you need for $1,300
a piece, and then you receive a tender from another supplier for a price of $200 a piece.
You are prone to see the second tender as cheap.
You decide to go with the second supplier and miss out on serious quality problems with their parts.
How to manage.
The best tip here is to take your time and ask for more time if possible.
Anchoring bias easily takes over when you feel rushed for time, which may cause you to make a decision
based on a first impression.
Overconfidence bias description.
This happens when you put too much faith in your own knowledge, experience, and opinions.
It may also lead you to think your contribution is more important than it actually is.
Example.
Your team needs to develop an upgrade to a software service you offer.
You feel so confident, especially in your own, but also your team's abilities that you decide on a
very short time frame to complete the upgrade.
The project runs over time, causing severe financial losses for your organization.
How to manage work on gathering comprehensive, objective data.
Seek out fact based sources to avoid relying on hunches, and get others involved in gathering information
in a systematic way.
Ask yourself how you can make the information or data more objective.
Availability bias description the tendency to overestimate the likelihood of an event occurring because
you remember it clearly from last time it happened.
It is easily available in your mind because it was recent, unusual, or emotionally charged.
Example.
You're going to a meeting with a large potential customer.
In the past few weeks, you have seen excessive coverage on the news and social media about train crashes
that use stories and vivid images to evoke an emotional response.
You therefore decide to fly instead of taking the train to the meeting, forgetting to fact check that
trains are actually statistically safer than both planes and cars.
When the potential client finds out about your choice, they do not wish to hire your company's services,
as their environmental policy does not encourage flying.
How to manage doubt your first answer.
No matter how good it seems, consider other alternatives thoroughly.
Even if you end up going back to your original answer.
Bandwagon bias description.
This is similar to so-called groupthink.
It means you are more likely to form an opinion or take an action, because other people around you
have already done so.
You hop on the bandwagon, so to speak.
Example you're a graphical designer looking to buy a new computer and decide to buy a mac, since everyone
knows they're the best computers for designers.
In fact, the modern PC with appropriate software would have worked great in your case for your needs
and would have integrated more seamlessly with all the other PC users in your organization.
But you did not take the time and effort to find that out.
How to manage.
Surround yourself with a diverse group of people who can bring fresh perspectives and are able to consider
ideas objectively.
Possibly use techniques that allow for everyone to submit ideas individually, and then share these
ideas with the group in an anonymized way.
Familiarity bias description.
The tendency to express an unreasonable amount of support for an idea, trait, or opinion simply because
you are familiar with it.
This can cause you to miss great ideas or people because you feel unfamiliar with them.
Example A local supplier offers the hospital you work at a better price for examination gloves, but
you decide to restock from the same supplier that you had previously ordered from.
Because you are more familiar with them and their products, you neglect to take the time to test that
the local suppliers products are actually higher quality, in addition to being better priced.
How to manage.
Think carefully about ideas, data, or individuals that you're already familiar with, even if you
have only encountered them briefly before.
Analyze why you would want to support that idea, data or individual?
Are you supporting it simply because it's familiar, or is it truly in line with what you think is best?
