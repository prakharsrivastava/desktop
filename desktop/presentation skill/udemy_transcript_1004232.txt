--- Setting Clear Objectives (Promise Value, Set Expectations) ---

Have you ever sat through a
presentation and after ten minutes,
you find yourself wondering…
"What is the point of all this?"
You don't know where it's going, or
what you're supposed to get out of it.
It feels like a random collection of facts.
This is a super common problem,
and our research shows it often starts
because the presenter themselves
isn't crystal clear on their goals.
This leads to objectives
that are "vague," "unclear," or
"not measurable." An Objectives
slide is your chance to
fix this right from the start.
It’s a contract with your audience.
It says, "Here is what I promise
you will be able to do or understand
by the end of our time together."
It gives them a reason to pay
attention. But it can't be a boring list.
Let's look at how to make
it compelling. This slide
doesn't just list goals; it presents
them as valuable outcomes.
It’s clear, focused, and
it sets a confident tone
for the entire presentation.
Let's break down the
psychology and design at play.
First, and most obviously,
we are using the Rule of Three.
We have exactly three goals.
Why?
Because the human brain
loves the number three.
It feels complete and
is incredibly memorable.
This is a direct application of Hick's
Law—by limiting the choices, we
make the message easy to absorb.
If we had listed seven objectives,
the audience would remember zero.
With three, they are much more
likely to retain them. Next, notice
how the middle card is slightly
larger and uses our main accent color.
This is a powerful persuasion principle
called the Von Restorff
Effect, or the Isolation Effect.
It states that the one
item that is different from
the rest is the most
likely to be remembered.
Here, we're visually
telling the audience, "All
three of these goals are important,
but this one in the middle—building
the slides—is the absolute
core of what we're doing today."
Within each card, we're using
principles we've already learned.
The icon and the text are grouped
closely together, using Proximity.
And the icon provides a
visual anchor for the text,
leveraging Dual Coding
Theory to make the goal
more understandable and memorable.
Finally, let's talk about the language.
This is super important.
We didn't write "I will
talk about principles."
We wrote "Master Core Principles."
We used strong, active verbs.
We framed the objectives around
what the audience will achieve.
This is the key to moving from
a vague, presenter-focused list
to a clear, audience-centric set of goals.
To create a powerful Objectives
slide, you need to: Distill your goals
down to the three most critical
outcomes using the Rule of Three.
Use a design trick like
the Von Restorff Effect
to highlight your most important point.
And frame your objectives
with strong, active language
that focuses on audience value.
This slide isn't just an outline;
it's a tool for building anticipation
and proving to your audience
that their time will be well spent.

we'll build this layout
and create these visually
distinct cards from scratch.
